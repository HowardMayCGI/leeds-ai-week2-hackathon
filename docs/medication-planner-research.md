# Medication planner: clinical and regulatory research

Research date: 24 October 2026. This note distinguishes published guidance from product recommendations. It is feasibility research, not patient-specific medication advice or a regulatory determination.

## Recommended direction

Keep the idea, but initially position it as a **pharmacist-reviewed medicine routine planner**: translate an existing, reconciled prescription into a practical timetable around the person's routine. Do not promise that generating a timetable makes the underlying prescription safe. Treat a clinician polypharmacy review as a later, separately evaluated product scope.

Recommended architecture: verified medicine instructions and patient information → explicit clinical constraints → deterministic scheduling → independent checks → pharmacist approval → understandable patient display. An LLM can assist with input extraction, clarification questions and explanation, with human confirmation of clinically meaningful fields. There is no inherent need to train a new machine-learning scheduler: scheduling known constraints is an optimisation problem. These are engineering recommendations, not requirements quoted from a regulator.

The intended setting is **inside the NHS App, using prescriptions in the patient's record**. That improves input provenance and avoids relying on patients retyping their prescriptions. For safety design, verify the coverage and freshness of the connected records, including hospital/private prescriptions, recent changes, over-the-counter medicines and supplements, and confirm ambiguous administration directions. Record access is an enabling integration, not a complete medicines-reconciliation process. This is a design recommendation; the precise NHS App integration and available data need separate verification.

## Clinical findings

- **Timing and interaction safety are different questions.** NHS guidance identifies NSAIDs such as ibuprofen as medicines that can increase warfarin's anticoagulant effect. A timetable should not infer that moving two medicines to different times makes their combined use safe. That last sentence is the clinical-design implication, not a direct quote from the source. [NHS anticoagulant considerations](https://www.nhs.uk/medicines/anticoagulants/considerations/)
- **Food rules require medicine-specific interpretation.** SPS points to product-characteristic administration, interaction and pharmacokinetic sections, the BNF and Stockley's. It warns that food affects medicines differently and that narrow therapeutic index medicines need particular care and consistency. A universal rule such as “all empty-stomach medicines go before breakfast” is inadequate. [SPS food administration guidance](https://sps.nhs.uk/articles/checking-if-medicines-can-be-given-with-food/)
- **Daily calendars must represent non-daily treatment.** Methotrexate for inflammatory conditions is commonly weekly; NHS guidance warns that taking it daily rather than weekly is dangerous and excessive doses can be fatal. This is a good synthetic negative test and a poor choice for autonomous hackathon scheduling. Indication matters: cancer regimens differ. [NHS methotrexate administration](https://www.nhs.uk/medicines/methotrexate/how-and-when-to-take-methotrexate/)
- **Some administration times are critical.** SPS maintains a time-critical medicines safety programme and resources covering, among others, insulin and Parkinson's medicines. A general “morning/lunch/evening” view cannot automatically replace a prescribed schedule. [SPS time-critical medicines](https://sps.nhs.uk/category/guidance/time-critical-medicines/)
- **Polypharmacy review is broader than pairwise drug interactions.** NICE's multimorbidity guidance focuses on treatment burden, patient priorities, harms and benefits; it recommends considering tools such as STOPP/START and both medicines that could be stopped and medicines that may be missing. Counting medicines or flagging interaction pairs is not an adequate proxy for appropriate prescribing. [NICE NG56 overview](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng56), [NG56 recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng56/chapter/recommendations)

Further design hazards to review with a pharmacist: wrong drug/strength/formulation, duplicate ingredients, missing over-the-counter drugs and supplements, obsolete medication lists, renal/hepatic impairment, allergies, pregnancy, variable doses, as-needed treatment, minimum intervals, overnight intervals, start/stop dates, missed-dose advice, and changes after discharge. These are proposed hazard-analysis topics, not a complete clinical specification.

## UK and NHS assurance

**Intended purpose drives the regulatory question.** MHRA software guidance explicitly identifies influencing treatment timing among functions likely to be medical devices. Displaying an existing instruction and making a new personalised treatment recommendation may therefore sit on different sides of a regulatory boundary. Human review does not automatically make clinical decision support exempt; determine the actual intended function, users, population and environment. [MHRA software guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/64a7d22d7a4c230013bba33c/Medical_device_stand-alone_software_including_apps__including_IVDMDs_.pdf), [MHRA intended-purpose guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/crafting-an-intended-purpose-in-the-context-of-software-as-a-medical-device-samd)

**Version control is necessary engineering discipline, not sufficient regulatory assurance.** MHRA's joint change-control principles emphasise bounded modifications, verification and validation, risk management, evidence and ongoing monitoring. Replacing a hosted LLM with a bespoke model does not remove these needs. Recommended audit information includes application release, model identifier, prompt/configuration, retrieved source versions, rule-set version, input data, output, validation results and approval. Re-run clinically justified regression evaluation before a change is released; do not infer reproducibility or safety from a model version alone. [MHRA/FDA/Health Canada change-control principles](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/predetermined-change-control-plans-for-machine-learning-enabled-medical-devices-guiding-principles/predetermined-change-control-plans-for-machine-learning-enabled-medical-devices-guiding-principles)

**NHS adoption adds clinical-safety and procurement assurance.** DCB0129 concerns manufacture of health IT and DCB0160 concerns deployment and use. They involve a Clinical Safety Officer, risk assessment and clinical safety documentation such as a hazard log and safety case. NHS England is reviewing these standards; check current versions at the point of deployment. [NHS clinical safety guidance](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/digital-clinical-safety-assurance/), [NHS developer clinical safety guide](https://digital.nhs.uk/developer/guides-and-documentation/introduction-to-healthcare-technology/clinical-safety)

DTAC covers clinical safety, data protection, technical security, interoperability, usability and accessibility. It applies alongside other required approvals and does not replace medical-device certification. NHS England revised DTAC in 2026; the previous form should not be used from 6 April 2026. [Current DTAC guidance](https://digital.nhs.uk/services/digital-technology-assessment-criteria-dtac)

Identifiable health information is special-category personal data. Establish a lawful basis and relevant special-category condition, assess processor arrangements and retention, and assess whether a DPIA is required before real-world processing. Synthetic patients are the sensible hackathon default. [ICO health-data definition](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/lawful-basis/special-category-data/what-is-special-category-data/), [ICO DPIA criteria](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/accountability-and-governance/data-protection-impact-assessments-dpias/when-do-we-need-to-do-a-dpia/)

## Hackathon scope and evaluation recommendations

1. Use a small pharmacist-approved set of synthetic adult cases and explicit, supplied prescription instructions. Keep doses and frequencies fixed.
2. Ask for waking, meal and sleeping times; propose an achievable routine and explain the source of each constraint.
3. Show missing information, conflicting instructions and cases with no valid schedule. Do not silently relax a clinical constraint or mark an unsupported case safe.
4. Make pharmacist review concrete: original instructions, extracted fields, chosen times, constraints satisfied, unresolved issues and dated approval.
5. Demonstrate one successful case, one ambiguous input and one deliberately unsatisfiable case. Exclude or refer complex/high-risk regimens rather than pretending to support them.
6. Evaluate prescription fidelity, omitted/extra doses, timing violations, detection of unsupported cases, reviewer effort and patient understanding. A visually plausible schedule is not a safety metric.

An AI checker may contribute additional warnings, but two AI calls may share errors. A deterministic checker only assures compliance with the rules and facts actually encoded; it cannot establish that the medicine list is complete, the rules clinically sufficient or the prescription appropriate. Pharmacist partnership and well-defined scope are more valuable early milestones than training a replacement model.

## Existing products, infrastructure and research

- **NHS App:** its documented prescription features already include acute and repeat prescription details. This supports the proposed starting point, but the documented view has limitations, including some hospital prescriptions. NHS App integration has a formal onboarding and standards process; it should be simulated for the hackathon rather than assumed to be available to this project. [Prescription features](https://digital.nhs.uk/services/nhs-app/nhs-app-features/prescriptions-in-the-nhs-app), [integration guidance](https://digital.nhs.uk/services/nhs-app/how-to-integrate-with-the-nhs-app)
- **Consumer tools:** Apple Health supports user-configured schedules, reminders, dose logging and export; Apple documents drug-interaction features as US-only. Medisafe and MyTherapy also offer medication-management/reminder workflows. These demonstrate an established product category, rather than proving a specific opportunity for autonomous scheduling has been solved. [Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-gb/105064), [Medisafe](https://app.medisafe.com/tips/welcome/index.php), [MyTherapy](https://production.mytherapyapp.com/)
- **Clinical knowledge products:** FDB Multilex offers UK prescribing decision support including interactions, duplicate therapies, dosing support and web API access. Stockley's Interactions Checker provides interaction information and management advice for clinicians. Investigate licensing, supported use and data coverage before integrating either; neither removes the need to validate the complete application. [FDB](https://www.fdbhealth.co.uk/solutions/multilex-clinical-decision-support), [Stockley's](https://www.pharmaceuticalpress.com/content/stockleys-interactions-checker/)
- **Medicine identifiers:** NHS dm+d provides standard codes and descriptions for medicines, including distinctions between formulations. NHS guidance explicitly says it does not provide decision-support functionality. Use it for identity/interoperability rather than treating it as an interaction or scheduling rule database. [NHS dm+d](https://digital.nhs.uk/services/terminology-and-classifications/dm-d)
- **MEDIC, Nature Medicine (2024):** the investigators combined an LLM with domain knowledge, pharmacy logic and guardrails to process medication directions. They reported a 33% reduction in near-miss events (95% CI 26–40%) during an online-pharmacy deployment. This supports studying bounded AI assistance in an existing pharmacy workflow; it does not establish safety for autonomous multi-medicine patient scheduling. [Primary study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11186789/)
- **SIMPLER trial:** pharmacist-led medication regimen simplification reduced daily administration times, but a subsequent analysis did not find a statistically significant difference in medication incidents between intervention and comparison groups. Distinguish practical simplification from a demonstrated reduction in harm, and note the Australian residential-care setting. [Primary trial analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7961370/)
- **LLM medication-task evaluation (2026):** one GPT-4o study reported 54.7% accuracy for identifying the interacting drug pair in its benchmark, alongside formulation and medication-order errors. This is a warning about relying on an unvalidated general model; it is not an error-rate estimate for every model or this proposed system. [Primary study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42717660/)

NICE explicitly recommends medicines reconciliation, including prescribed, over-the-counter and complementary medicines, with patient/carer involvement. NHS England's structured medication reviews already provide a clinical workflow to which a planner could attach. A pharmacist-approved plan delivered through the NHS App is therefore a credible initial product hypothesis. [NICE NG5](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng5/chapter/Recommendations), [NHS structured medication reviews](https://www.england.nhs.uk/primary-care/pharmacy/smr/)
